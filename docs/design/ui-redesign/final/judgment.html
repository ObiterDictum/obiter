Rock Advertising Ltd v MWB Business Exchange Centres Ltd

[2018] UKSC 24 · 16 May 2018 · Neutral citation

Coram: Lord Neuberger, Lady Hale, Lord Wilson, Lord Sumption and Lord Briggs. Lead judgment by Lord Sumption; Lord Briggs gave a concurring judgment.

Illustrative extract The paragraphs below reconstruct the judgment’s reasoning for this prototype. They are not the certified text. The original is on The National Archives (Find Case Law). Open original

Introduction

1

LORD SUMPTION (with whom Lord Neuberger, Lady Hale and Lord Wilson agree): This appeal is about the effectiveness of a clause in a written contract which provides that the contract may not be varied except in writing signed by both parties. The immediate question is whether an oral variation of the contract is effective despite such a clause.

2

The respondent, Rock Advertising Ltd (“Rock”), carries on a marketing business. It occupied office space in London under a licence agreement with the appellant, MWB Business Exchange Centres Ltd (“MWB”).

3

The licence fee was payable monthly in advance. Rock fell into arrears, and MWB had locked Rock out of the premises.

The facts

4

Rock’s director proposed to MWB’s credit controller, in a telephone conversation, a revised payment schedule under which the arrears would be cleared over several months.

5

The judge found that the parties had reached an oral agreement to vary the licence by rescheduling the arrears. If the oral variation was effective, the money claimed by MWB was not due.

The clause

6

Clause 7.6 of the licence required all variations to be agreed, set out in writing and signed on behalf of both parties before they took effect.

7

The Court of Appeal held that clause 7.6 did not prevent the oral variation from taking effect. In its view a party could always waive a provision inserted for its own benefit, and the ordinary principles of estoppel and agreement applied.

The appeal

8

The appeal raises two questions. The first is whether clause 7.6 is effective to prevent variation otherwise than in the manner it prescribes. The second is whether the oral variation was supported by consideration.

9

In my view the first question must be answered in favour of MWB. The oral variation on which Rock relies was ineffective because it did not comply with clause 7.6. That conclusion makes it unnecessary to decide the second question.

10

The starting point is the principle of party autonomy. There is no reason in principle why parties who are capable of agreeing a contract should not agree to impose a formal requirement on the manner in which they may later vary it. Such clauses are commonplace in commercial contracts, and they serve legitimate purposes: they prevent inadvertent changes being made by junior staff, and they ensure that a written record exists.

11

It is true that the law of contract does not generally require writing, and that a clause prohibiting oral variation may in some circumstances be displaced by an estoppel or a subsequent agreement. But the mere fact that the parties have acted on an oral agreement will not ordinarily be enough. Something more is required, such as a representation or conduct which makes it unconscionable for the party relying on the clause to insist on it.

12

The judge made no finding of estoppel. Nor did the Court of Appeal identify any conduct which would make it unconscionable for MWB to rely on clause 7.6. The oral variation was therefore ineffective, and the arrears remained due.

Disposition

13

For these reasons I would allow the appeal and set aside the order of the Court of Appeal.

14

The oral variation was ineffective. I would add that nothing in this judgment prevents parties from agreeing a written variation which complies with the contract, nor from agreeing to dispense with the clause for the future.
